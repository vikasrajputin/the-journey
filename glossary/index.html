Glossary

Words worth knowing

Plain-language definitions of the terms used across the exhibition. In the chapters, underlined terms open these definitions in place.

ABCDEGHILMNOPRSTZ

A

accretion
The gradual growth of a body as smaller pieces collide and stick to it. Planets grew by accretion from dust, pebbles and larger rocky bodies.
ancient DNA
Genetic material recovered from old bones, teeth or sediments. It is usually damaged and fragmentary, so special methods are needed to read it reliably.

B

BCE / CE
Before the Common Era and Common Era: a calendar convention numbering years from a traditional starting point. It is widely used today, but ancient societies kept their own calendars.
Big Bang model
The scientific description of the universe developing from an extremely hot, dense early state and expanding ever since. It describes what happened after that early state, not what, if anything, came before it.

C

Cambrian diversification
The appearance of most major animal groups in the fossil record between roughly 539 and 500 million years ago. It built on earlier animal life and unfolded over tens of millions of years.
common ancestor
A population from which two or more later groups are descended. Humans and chimpanzees share a common ancestor; neither descends from the other.
cosmic microwave background
Faint microwave radiation that fills the sky in every direction. It is light released about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, when the universe first became transparent.
cumulative culture
Knowledge and skills that build up across generations, with each generation learning from and improving on what came before.
cuneiform
A writing system of wedge-shaped marks pressed into clay, developed in Mesopotamia. It was used for several languages over about 3,000 years.

D

dark matter
Matter that does not emit or absorb light but whose gravity shapes galaxies and the large-scale structure of the universe. What it is made of is still unknown.
Denisovans
A hominin group known mainly from DNA and a small number of fossils from Asia. Some living populations, especially in Oceania, carry Denisovan DNA.
differentiation
The separation of a young planet into layers by density: heavy iron sank to form the core, while lighter rock formed the mantle and crust.
domestication
A long-term relationship in which people shape the breeding of plants or animals, which change genetically as a result. It usually took centuries to millennia.

E

endosymbiosis
One organism living inside another to the benefit of both. Mitochondria and chloroplasts are thought to descend from bacteria that once lived inside other cells.
eukaryote
An organism whose cells keep their DNA inside a nucleus and contain other internal compartments. Animals, plants, fungi and many single-celled organisms are eukaryotes.
expansion of space
The steady increase in distances between galaxies that are not bound together by gravity. Space itself stretches; galaxies are not flying apart through a pre-existing void.

G

galaxy
A vast system of stars, gas, dust and dark matter held together by gravity. Our galaxy, the Milky Way, contains hundreds of billions of stars.
genetic drift
Random changes in how common gene variants are in a population, especially strong in small populations. Unlike natural selection, it is driven by chance.
Great Oxidation Event
A period beginning roughly 2.4 billion years ago when oxygen first built up in Earth's atmosphere. It unfolded over a long time and unevenly, rather than in a single moment.

H

habitability
Whether an environment could support life as we know it, for example by having liquid water, energy sources and the right chemistry. Being habitable does not mean life must appear.
half-life
The time it takes for half of the radioactive atoms in a sample to decay. Each isotope has its own fixed half-life, from fractions of a second to billions of years.
heredity
The passing of traits from parents to offspring through genetic material, mostly DNA.
hominin
Humans and the extinct species more closely related to us than to chimpanzees and bonobos. The group has many branches, most of which died out.

I

industrialisation
The shift to producing goods with machines and large amounts of energy, beginning in Britain in the 1700s and spreading unevenly worldwide.
iridium anomaly
An unusually high concentration of the metal iridium in a thin rock layer. Iridium is rare in Earth's crust but more common in many asteroids.

L

language model
A machine-learning system trained on large amounts of text to predict likely next words. Such systems can produce fluent text but can also be wrong.

M

machine learning
Building computer systems that improve at a task by finding patterns in data rather than following only hand-written rules.
manuscript
A handwritten document. For many ancient works, the surviving manuscripts were copied centuries after the text was first composed.
mass extinction
A geologically brief interval in which a large share of the world's species die out. Five are usually recognised in the past 500 million years.
Mesozoic Era
The era from about 252 to 66 million years ago, divided into the Triassic, Jurassic and Cretaceous periods.
metabolism
The chemical reactions a living thing uses to capture energy and build or break down materials.
microbe
A living thing too small to see without a microscope, such as a bacterium or archaeon. Microbes were the only life on Earth for most of its history.
molecular clock
Estimating when lineages split from the number of genetic differences between them. It depends on assumptions about mutation rates and needs calibration with fossils.
multicellularity
Life made of many cooperating cells. It evolved independently several times, including in animals, plants, fungi and some algae.
mutation
A change in the DNA sequence. Most have little effect, some are harmful and a few are useful. Mutations are the ultimate source of new genetic variation.

N

natural selection
The process in which inherited traits that help organisms survive and reproduce in their environment become more common over generations. It has no goal or foresight.
Neanderthals
A hominin group that lived across Europe and western Asia until about 40,000 years ago. Many living people carry a small share of Neanderthal DNA.
Neolithic
A label for the period when farming and settled villages spread in a region. It began at different times in different places.
non-avian dinosaurs
All dinosaurs except birds. Non-avian dinosaurs died out 66 million years ago; birds survived.
nucleosynthesis
The making of atomic nuclei. Hydrogen, helium and a little lithium formed in the first minutes of the universe; most heavier elements were made later, in stars and stellar explosions.

O

observable universe
The part of the universe from which light has had time to reach us since the universe began. The whole universe may be much larger, or even infinite.
oral transmission
Passing on texts, stories or knowledge by speech and memory rather than in writing. Many traditions were transmitted orally for generations before being written down.

P

peer review
Evaluation of research by other experts before publication. It catches many errors but does not guarantee that a result is correct.
photosynthesis
Using sunlight to make food from carbon dioxide and water. The kind used by cyanobacteria, algae and plants releases oxygen as a by-product.
plate tectonics
The slow movement of the large rigid plates that make up Earth's outer shell. Plates spread apart, collide and sink, recycling the surface over hundreds of millions of years.
primate
The group of mammals that includes lemurs, monkeys, apes and humans.
prokaryote
A single-celled organism whose DNA is not enclosed in a nucleus. Bacteria and archaea are prokaryotes.
proxy data
Indirect records of past conditions, such as ice cores, tree rings and sediment layers, used where no direct measurements exist.

R

radiocarbon dating
Dating once-living material from the decay of carbon-14. It works back to around 50,000 years at most, so it cannot date dinosaur fossils.
radiometric dating
Working out the age of a rock or object from the steady decay of radioactive atoms it contains, using their known half-lives.
redshift
The stretching of light to longer, redder wavelengths. Light from distant galaxies is redshifted because space expanded while the light was travelling to us.

S

scientific methods
Ways of testing ideas against evidence: observing, measuring, experimenting, sharing results and having others check and try to repeat them.
shocked quartz
Quartz grains with microscopic deformation features formed only under extreme, sudden pressure, such as a large impact.
stratigraphy
The study of rock or soil layers. In undisturbed layers, lower layers were laid down first, which lets scientists put events in order.
stromatolite
A layered rock structure built up by communities of microbes trapping sediment or causing minerals to form. Living examples still grow today, for instance in Shark Bay, Australia.

T

taphonomy
The study of what happens to an organism after death, including how it might become a fossil. It explains why the fossil record is patchy.
tetrapod
A vertebrate with four limbs, or descended from ancestors that had them. Amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals are tetrapods.
textual criticism
Comparing surviving copies of a text to understand how it changed over time and, where possible, what earlier versions said.
transitional fossil
A fossil showing a mix of features from an earlier group and a later one. It records a stage in a branching history, not necessarily a direct ancestor.

Z

zircon
A tough mineral crystal that traps uranium but not lead when it forms. This makes zircons excellent natural clocks, and some have survived for over 4 billion years.
