The World We Built

Methods, machines, energy and networks, and what they cost

Our biology barely changed. What changed was what we could build on each other's work.

When
From roughly the 1500s CE to the present
Where
Worldwide, but unevenly
Scale
From a few million people with printed books to eight billion connected by global networks

▶ Experience this chapter

See this chapter on the full timeline →

In the last few centuries, human knowledge and technology have changed faster than ever before. That acceleration was not caused by a sudden change in human brains. It grew from cumulative culture, writing, institutions, trade, energy and communication, and it brought both enormous benefits and serious harms.

Skip to the evidence for this chapter

01

Why change sped up

People 500 years ago were biologically much like us. What changed was the scale of . Printing made texts cheaper and more uniform. Universities, scientific societies and journals created communities that shared, checked and built on each other's results. Expanding trade connected regions and moved goods, people and ideas. Above all, new sources of energy multiplied what human and animal muscle could do.

02

New ways of knowing

In 1610, Galileo published telescope observations of the Moon and of moons orbiting Jupiter. Instruments like the telescope and microscope revealed worlds invisible to the naked eye. Over the following centuries, matured: careful measurement, controlled experiments, mathematical models, publication, attempts to replicate results and open criticism by other experts, including .

These methods are powerful because they are designed to catch mistakes, including the investigator's own. They do not make scientists infallible, and science has often been entangled with the prejudices and politics of its time. But over time, the practice of testing ideas against evidence has corrected many errors.

03

From the 1700s

Energy and industry

Steam engines, improved by James Watt and others in the 1700s, turned the energy stored in coal into mechanical work. began in Britain and spread, unevenly, across the world. Later came oil, electricity, the internal combustion engine and, in the twentieth century, nuclear power and modern renewables.

Global energy use has grown many times over in the last two centuries, mostly from fossil fuels. Estimates for earlier years are reconstructions with considerable uncertainty, but the overall pattern of steep growth is clear.

04

Longer, healthier lives

Understanding that many diseases are caused by microbes led to clean water supplies, sewers, antiseptic surgery, vaccines and antibiotics. Global average life expectancy has more than doubled since 1900, largely because far fewer children now die young. Those gains have not been shared equally between or within countries.

05

Computers and networks

Electronic computers emerged in the 1940s. Transistors and integrated circuits made them smaller, cheaper and vastly more powerful. Computer networks followed, and in 1989 Tim Berners-Lee, working at CERN, proposed the system that became the World Wide Web.

Today, billions of people carry networked computers in their pockets. Information that once took months to cross an ocean now crosses it in a fraction of a second.

06

Artificial intelligence, without the hype

Modern artificial intelligence mostly means : systems trained on large amounts of data to perform tasks such as prediction, classification, recognising patterns in images or sound, and generating text or pictures. A learns statistical patterns in text and uses them to produce new text.

Capabilities vary widely between systems and tasks. AI systems can be very useful, but they can also be wrong, biased or misused, and their effects on work, information and power are still unfolding. Governments, companies and researchers are developing standards and laws to manage these risks; how best to govern AI is an open and actively debated question.

07

Uneven progress, real costs

The modern world was not built evenly or innocently. Industrialisation and global trade were bound up with colonialism, slavery and the exploitation of labour and land. Technologies that improved many lives were also used in war. Access to their benefits remains deeply unequal.

Human activity now shapes the Earth system itself. According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, human activities, principally through emissions of greenhouse gases, have unequivocally caused global warming. Land use, pollution and biodiversity loss are also reshaping ecosystems worldwide.

How do we know?

Evidence we have

The main kinds of evidence behind this chapter, what each can and cannot tell us, and how confident researchers are. How to read these labels.

●●○Strong evidence

Scientific publications and historical documents

What it is
Journals, letters, notebooks, government records and books.
What it helps us understand
Documents who discovered what, when, and how ideas were debated and accepted.
How it is studied
Historical research in archives and libraries.
What it cannot tell us
Records favour those with access to print and institutions; many contributors, including technicians and colonised peoples, are under-recorded.
●●○Strong evidence

Patents and technical artefacts

What it is
Surviving machines, instruments, prototypes and patent filings.
What it helps us understand
Shows how technologies actually worked and changed.
How it is studied
Museum conservation, reconstruction and technical analysis.
What it cannot tell us
Patents record claims, not always practical use; credit for inventions is often shared or disputed.
●●●Well established

Instrument records and datasets

What it is
Long-term measurements such as temperature records, atmospheric CO₂ and energy statistics.
What it helps us understand
Quantifies change over time.
How it is studied
Standardised instruments, quality control and statistical analysis.
What it cannot tell us
Older records are sparser and need careful adjustment; pre-modern values are reconstructions.
●●○Strong evidence

Environmental and demographic records

What it is
Census data, parish records, health statistics and environmental surveys.
What it helps us understand
Tracks population, life expectancy and environmental change.
How it is studied
Demography, epidemiology and environmental science.
What it cannot tell us
Coverage is patchy before the twentieth century and for many regions.

Why it isn't about bigger brains

People living 10,000 years ago were anatomically modern humans, and there is no evidence of a major change in human cognitive ability during recorded history. Technological acceleration is better explained by cumulative culture, larger and more connected populations, institutions that store and transmit knowledge, and access to energy.

Glossary

cumulative culture

Knowledge and skills that build up across generations, with each generation learning from and improving on what came before.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

scientific methods

Ways of testing ideas against evidence: observing, measuring, experimenting, sharing results and having others check and try to repeat them.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

peer review

Evaluation of research by other experts before publication. It catches many errors but does not guarantee that a result is correct.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

industrialisation

The shift to producing goods with machines and large amounts of energy, beginning in Britain in the 1700s and spreading unevenly worldwide.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

machine learning

Building computer systems that improve at a task by finding patterns in data rather than following only hand-written rules.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

language model

A machine-learning system trained on large amounts of text to predict likely next words. Such systems can produce fluent text but can also be wrong.
