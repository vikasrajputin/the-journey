Life's history is not a ladder. It is a tree, still growing in every direction.

When
Roughly 1 billion to 250 million years ago, focusing on the last 540 million years
Where
First in the oceans, then along shorelines, rivers and continents
Scale
From single cells to forests tens of metres tall

Over hundreds of millions of years, life branched into an astonishing variety of forms. Some lineages flourished, many vanished, and survivors repeatedly filled the empty spaces left behind.

01

How life changesHow life changes

Evolution is not a plan. It is what happens when living things reproduce with variation in a changing world.

  • Variation: individuals differ, partly because of differences in their genes.
  • Inheritance: many of those differences are passed to offspring.
  • : copying errors and other changes add new variation in every generation.
  • : variants that help survival and reproduction in a given environment tend to become more common.
  • : chance alone also changes which variants spread, especially in small populations.
  • Extinction: lineages that cannot persist disappear, which reshapes the tree.

Sources

Over many generations, populations that become separated can change in different directions until they form separate species. Trace any two living species back far enough and you reach a . All known life is connected in one family tree.

A common misconception

No organism is 'more evolved'

A bacterium living today has an ancestry exactly as long as yours. Evolution does not aim at humans or at complexity. Many lineages became simpler over time, and the most numerous organisms on Earth are still microbes.

02

Many cells working togetherMany cells working together

evolved more than once, in separate lineages including animals, plants, fungi and several groups of algae. Cells that stayed together could divide up work: some for feeding, some for protection, some for reproduction.

The first large, complex organisms in the fossil record appear in the Ediacaran Period, roughly 575 to 539 million years ago. Many of them were soft-bodied forms so unlike anything alive today that scientists still debate how they relate to later animals.

03

About 539 to 487 million years ago

The Cambrian diversificationThe Cambrian diversification

In the Cambrian Period, the fossil record fills with animals: creatures with shells, legs, eyes and guts, including early relatives of insects, crabs, worms, molluscs and our own vertebrate ancestors. Exceptionally preserved sites, such as the Burgess Shale in Canada, even capture soft bodies.

A common misconception

Not 'out of nowhere'

The so-called Cambrian explosion unfolded over tens of millions of years and built on earlier animal life. Part of its suddenness is about preservation: hard shells and skeletons fossilise far more easily than soft bodies, so animals became much more visible in the rocks once they evolved hard parts.

04

Life moves onto landLife moves onto land

Moving from water to land was not one event but many, spread over more than 100 million years. Microbes and probably simple plants and fungi came first. Land plants gradually evolved waxy coatings, spores, internal plumbing and eventually roots, wood and seeds. By the Devonian Period, about 420 to 359 million years ago, the first forests had appeared.

Arthropods, the group that includes insects, spiders and millipedes, were among the earliest animals on land. Vertebrates followed. Fossils such as Tiktaalik, a Devonian fish with sturdy, limb-like fins, a flexible neck and lungs as well as gills, capture stages in the evolution of : four-limbed animals.

From early tetrapods came amphibians, and from them the first animals to lay eggs with protective membranes, which freed them from returning to water to breed. Those early amniotes gave rise to the lineages that became reptiles, birds and mammals.

05

Loss and recoveryLoss and recovery

Most species that have ever lived are now extinct. Extinction is a normal part of life's history, but five times in the last half-billion years it has been catastrophic. In a , a large share of species disappear in a geologically short time, often linked to rapid changes in climate and ocean chemistry.

The largest, at the end of the Permian Period about 252 million years ago, wiped out the great majority of marine species. Massive volcanic eruptions in what is now Siberia are widely thought to have played a central role.

How do we know?

Evidence we have

The main kinds of evidence behind this chapter, what each can and cannot tell us, and how confident researchers are. How to read these labels.

●●●Well established

Fossil succession and transitional fossils

What it is
Fossils found in a consistent order through rock layers worldwide, including forms with mixtures of older and newer features.
What it helps us understand
Shows the sequence in which groups appeared and records intermediate stages, such as fish with limb-like fins.
How it is studied
Excavation, comparative description and placement within dated rock sequences.
What it cannot tell us
Fossilisation is rare and biased towards hard parts and certain environments; most species left no fossils.
●●●Well established

Comparative anatomy

What it is
Shared underlying structures in different organisms, such as the same arrangement of bones in a human arm, a bat wing and a whale flipper.
What it helps us understand
Reveals common ancestry beneath very different ways of life.
How it is studied
Dissection, imaging and comparison of living and fossil organisms.
What it cannot tell us
Similar features can also evolve independently in unrelated groups, so anatomy is combined with other evidence.
●●●Well established

DNA and molecular phylogenetics

What it is
Comparisons of DNA and protein sequences between species.
What it helps us understand
Builds family trees independently of fossils, and these largely agree with anatomy and the fossil record.
How it is studied
Sequencing genomes and building statistical trees.
What it cannot tell us
Dating branch points uses molecular clocks that need fossil calibration and can carry large uncertainties.
●●●Well established

Stratigraphy and radiometric dating

What it is
The ordering of rock layers combined with radiometric ages from volcanic layers within them.
What it helps us understand
Places fossils and events on a numeric timeline, such as the start of the Cambrian about 539 million years ago.
How it is studied
Field mapping, correlating layers between regions, and dating volcanic ash beds.
What it cannot tell us
Not every layer can be dated directly; ages between dated layers are interpolated.

What a transitional fossil is, and isn't

A shows a combination of features from an earlier and a later group. Tiktaalik is not necessarily the direct ancestor of four-legged animals; it is better seen as a close relative of that ancestor, showing what animals at that stage of the branching tree looked like.

Glossary

mutation

A change in the DNA sequence. Most have little effect, some are harmful and a few are useful. Mutations are the ultimate source of new genetic variation.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

natural selection

The process in which inherited traits that help organisms survive and reproduce in their environment become more common over generations. It has no goal or foresight.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

genetic drift

Random changes in how common gene variants are in a population, especially strong in small populations. Unlike natural selection, it is driven by chance.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

common ancestor

A population from which two or more later groups are descended. Humans and chimpanzees share a common ancestor; neither descends from the other.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

multicellularity

Life made of many cooperating cells. It evolved independently several times, including in animals, plants, fungi and some algae.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

tetrapod

A vertebrate with four limbs, or descended from ancestors that had them. Amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals are tetrapods.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

mass extinction

A geologically brief interval in which a large share of the world's species die out. Five are usually recognised in the past 500 million years.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

transitional fossil

A fossil showing a mix of features from an earlier group and a later one. It records a stage in a branching history, not necessarily a direct ancestor.
