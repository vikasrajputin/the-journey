A Planet Is BornA Planet Is Born

How a cloud of dust became a living, restless world

Earth was not made for life. It became a place where life was possible.

When
About 4.6 to 4 billion years ago, with changes continuing to the present
Where
A spiral arm of the Milky Way, about two-thirds of the way out from its centre
Scale
Earth is about 12,700 kilometres across, and about 150 million kilometres from the Sun

▶ Experience this chapter

See this chapter on the full timeline →

More than nine billion years after the Big Bang, in an unremarkable part of an ordinary galaxy, a cloud of gas and dust began to collapse. From it came the Sun, the planets, and the only world where we know life exists.

Skip to the evidence for this chapter

01

About 4.6 billion years ago

A collapsing cloud

The Solar System formed from a rotating cloud of gas and dust about 4.6 billion years ago. As the cloud collapsed under its own gravity, it spun faster and flattened into a disc. Most of the material fell to the centre, where it became hot and dense enough for fusion: the Sun was born.

That cloud already contained elements made by earlier generations of stars. Without them, there would be no rock, no metal and no water to build planets from.

02

Building a planet by collision

In the disc around the young Sun, dust grains stuck together and grew. Through , pebbles became boulders, boulders became bodies kilometres across, and those collided and merged into planets. Earth reached roughly its present size within tens of millions of years.

We can date this remarkably well. Using of meteorites, which are leftover fragments from the Solar System's formation, together with the oldest Earth and Moon rocks, scientists estimate that Earth is about 4.54 billion years old, give or take about 50 million years.

03

A world of fire, and the birth of the Moon

Young Earth was hot. Heat from collisions and from radioactive elements melted much of the planet. The early surface may have been, at times, an ocean of molten rock.

The leading explanation for the Moon's origin is a giant impact. In this model, a body roughly the size of Mars collided with the young Earth, and debris thrown into orbit gathered to form the Moon. It explains much of what we know: the Moon's relatively small iron core, its chemical similarity to Earth's mantle, and the large spin of the Earth–Moon system.

What we don't know yet

A model, not an observation

Nobody saw the Moon form. The giant-impact model is our best explanation of the evidence, and it is supported by rock samples brought back by the Apollo missions and by computer simulations. Details remain debated, including the size of the impactor and why Earth and Moon rocks are so chemically alike.

04

Sorting into layers

While Earth was largely molten, heavy iron and nickel sank towards the centre to form the core, and lighter rock rose to form the mantle and crust. This gave Earth the layered structure it has today, including a molten outer core whose motion generates the magnetic field that helps shield the surface from charged particles from the Sun.

05

Oceans and air

As the surface cooled, water vapour released from inside the planet and delivered by impacts could condense. Tiny crystals about 4.4 billion years old carry chemical hints that liquid water may have existed at the surface very early, though this interpretation is still discussed.

The early atmosphere was very different from today's. It contained little or no free oxygen, and was probably dominated by gases such as carbon dioxide, nitrogen and water vapour. How much water came from Earth's own rocks and how much from asteroids or comets, and exactly when the first lasting oceans formed, are still active research questions.

06

A restless surface

Earth's outer shell is broken into large plates that move a few centimetres a year, about as fast as fingernails grow. Through , new crust forms at ocean ridges, old crust sinks back into the mantle, and continents drift, collide and split apart. Over hundreds of millions of years, the surface is recycled again and again.

This recycling is one reason why very old rocks are rare. It also matters for life: over long timescales, tectonics and volcanism help cycle carbon between rocks, oceans and air, which influences climate. When plate tectonics in its modern form began is debated.

07

Habitable, but not destined

By around four billion years ago, Earth probably had liquid water, sources of chemical energy and a range of environments, from shallow seas to hot springs. That made it : capable of supporting life as we know it.

A common misconception

Habitable does not mean inevitable

It is tempting to see early Earth as a world set up for life. Scientists avoid that framing. Conditions made life possible; they did not make it certain. Whether life arises easily wherever conditions allow is one of the biggest open questions in science.

How do we know?

Evidence we have

The main kinds of evidence behind this chapter, what each can and cannot tell us, and how confident researchers are. How to read these labels.

●●●Well established

Radiometric dating of meteorites and minerals

What it is
Ages measured from the decay of radioactive elements, such as uranium to lead, in meteorites and ancient minerals.
What it helps us understand
Gives Earth's age (about 4.54 billion years) and dates the Solar System's oldest solids.
How it is studied
Mass spectrometers measure parent and daughter isotopes in carefully selected samples.
What it cannot tell us
Works only when a sample has stayed chemically closed since it formed; geologists test for this, and dates are given with uncertainty ranges.
●○○Active research

Ancient zircon crystals

What it is
Tiny, tough mineral grains up to about 4.4 billion years old, found in younger rocks in Western Australia.
What it helps us understand
Provides the oldest direct material from Earth, with chemical clues about early crust and possibly water.
How it is studied
Uranium–lead dating and oxygen-isotope analysis of individual crystals.
What it cannot tell us
Each grain is a tiny fragment, removed from its original rock; what it says about the wider early Earth is debated.
●●○Strong evidence

Moon rocks and planetary science

What it is
Rock samples returned from the Moon, plus observations of other planets and young star systems.
What it helps us understand
Supports the giant-impact model of the Moon's origin and helps reconstruct how planets form.
How it is studied
Chemical and isotopic analysis of samples, spacecraft observations and computer simulations.
What it cannot tell us
Simulations depend on assumptions, and some chemical similarities between Earth and Moon are not yet fully explained.
●●●Well established

Earthquake waves and geological structures

What it is
The way seismic waves bend, slow and reflect inside Earth, plus rocks, faults and ocean-floor patterns at the surface.
What it helps us understand
Reveals Earth's layers and the movement of tectonic plates.
How it is studied
Global networks of seismometers, rock dating and mapping of the sea floor.
What it cannot tell us
Deep structure is inferred indirectly; no one has drilled beyond the crust.

Why date Earth using meteorites?

Earth's own earliest rocks were melted, eroded or recycled by plate tectonics long ago. Many meteorites, by contrast, are fragments that have barely changed since the Solar System formed. Because Earth and meteorites formed from the same disc at about the same time, their ages, combined with lead-isotope measurements on Earth, give a consistent age of about 4.54 billion years.

Glossary

accretion

The gradual growth of a body as smaller pieces collide and stick to it. Planets grew by accretion from dust, pebbles and larger rocky bodies.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

radiometric dating

Working out the age of a rock or object from the steady decay of radioactive atoms it contains, using their known half-lives.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

differentiation

The separation of a young planet into layers by density: heavy iron sank to form the core, while lighter rock formed the mantle and crust.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

zircon

A tough mineral crystal that traps uranium but not lead when it forms. This makes zircons excellent natural clocks, and some have survived for over 4 billion years.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

plate tectonics

The slow movement of the large rigid plates that make up Earth's outer shell. Plates spread apart, collide and sink, recycling the surface over hundreds of millions of years.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

habitability

Whether an environment could support life as we know it, for example by having liquid water, energy sources and the right chemistry. Being habitable does not mean life must appear.
