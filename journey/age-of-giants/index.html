The Age of GiantsThe Age of Giants

Dinosaurs, drifting continents, and the day the world changed

Dinosaurs did not all disappear. Look out of a window, and you may see one.

When
The Mesozoic Era, about 252 to 66 million years ago
Where
Every continent, as the supercontinent Pangaea broke apart
Scale
From sparrow-sized dinosaurs to long-necked giants over 30 metres long

▶ Experience this chapter

See this chapter on the full timeline →

For about 186 million years, the Mesozoic world was home to dinosaurs, marine reptiles, flying pterosaurs, early mammals and the first flowering plants. Then, about 66 million years ago, most of it ended.

01

Three periods, one era

The is divided into three periods: the Triassic (about 252 to 201 million years ago), the Jurassic (201 to 143) and the Cretaceous (143 to 66). Each lasted tens of millions of years. Tyrannosaurus lived closer in time to us than to Stegosaurus.

02

From about 230 million years ago

The rise of the dinosaurs

The earliest known dinosaurs appear in the Triassic, around 230 million years ago, in rocks from what are now South America and Africa. They were mostly modest-sized, two-legged animals, sharing the world with many other reptile groups, including the ancestors of crocodiles.

Over the following tens of millions of years, dinosaurs diversified into many ecological roles: giant plant-eating sauropods, armoured and horned herbivores, fast predators, and small feathered forms. They lived on every continent, including those that are now polar.

03

A world in motion

At the start of the Mesozoic, most land was joined in a single supercontinent, Pangaea. Over the era it broke apart, opening the Atlantic Ocean and separating the continents. Climates were generally warmer than today, often with little or no permanent polar ice, and sea levels were frequently higher.

As continents separated, animal and plant populations became isolated and evolved along different paths. That is one reason why late Cretaceous dinosaurs in North America differed from those in South America or Asia.

04

Not just dinosaurs

Mesozoic seas held marine reptiles such as ichthyosaurs, plesiosaurs and, later, mosasaurs, which were not dinosaurs. Pterosaurs, the first vertebrates to evolve powered flight, were close relatives of dinosaurs but not dinosaurs themselves. Insects flourished, and in the Cretaceous, flowering plants spread and diversified, transforming landscapes.

Mammals were there too. They first appeared around the same time as the dinosaurs and lived alongside them for well over 100 million years. Most were small, but recent finds show they had already evolved a variety of ways of life: burrowing, climbing, swimming and gliding.

05

Birds are living dinosaurs

Birds evolved from small, feathered, meat-eating dinosaurs called theropods, the same broad group that includes Tyrannosaurus and Velociraptor. Fossils from China and elsewhere show many non-avian dinosaurs with feathers, and birds share features such as hollow bones and wishbones with their dinosaur relatives.

A common misconception

The dinosaurs did not all die

The extinction 66 million years ago wiped out the non-avian dinosaurs: all dinosaurs except birds. Some birds survived, and today there are more than ten thousand bird species. In the scientific sense, dinosaurs are still with us.

06

About 66 million years ago

The end of the Cretaceous

In 1980, a team led by physicist Luis Alvarez and geologist Walter Alvarez reported a thin clay layer at the boundary between Cretaceous and later rocks, enriched in iridium: a metal rare in Earth's crust but more common in many asteroids. They proposed that a large asteroid had struck Earth. Later, the was found worldwide, along with and other impact debris.

The crater was identified at Chicxulub, buried beneath Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula. It is roughly 180 kilometres across, and its age matches the extinction layer, which radiometric dating places at about 66 million years ago. The impact would have caused fires, tsunamis and a global darkening that disrupted photosynthesis.

What we don't know yet

The role of volcanoes

At around the same time, enormous volcanic eruptions in what is now India, the Deccan Traps, released large amounts of gas. Most researchers see the impact as the main cause of the extinction, but scientists continue to study how much the volcanism stressed ecosystems before and after, and how the two may have interacted.

07

After the impact

Around three-quarters of species are estimated to have disappeared. The survivors included birds, mammals, crocodiles, turtles, many insects and plants. Over the following millions of years, mammals diversified into many of the ecological roles once filled by dinosaurs, eventually including primates.

How do we know?

Evidence we have

The main kinds of evidence behind this chapter, what each can and cannot tell us, and how confident researchers are. How to read these labels.

●●●Well established

Fossils and fossil-bearing rock layers

What it is
Bones, teeth, eggs, footprints, feathers and plant remains preserved in Mesozoic sedimentary rocks.
What it helps us understand
Reconstructs who lived where and when, and how they were related.
How it is studied
Excavation, anatomical comparison, CT scanning and analysis of the surrounding rock.
What it cannot tell us
Preservation favours large, hard-bodied animals in certain environments; colours and behaviour are usually inferred.
●●●Well established

The Chicxulub crater

What it is
A large buried impact structure in the Yucatán region of Mexico.
What it helps us understand
Identifies the impact site, matching the age of the extinction boundary.
How it is studied
Gravity and magnetic surveys, seismic imaging and drilled rock cores.
What it cannot tell us
The crater is buried, so its full structure is reconstructed from indirect measurements and limited drill cores.
●●●Well established

Iridium-rich boundary layer and shocked minerals

What it is
A thin, global clay layer with high iridium, shocked quartz and glassy spherules.
What it helps us understand
Records the debris of a large impact spread around the world.
How it is studied
Chemical analysis and microscopy of samples from many sites on land and in ocean sediment cores.
What it cannot tell us
Local erosion and mixing mean the layer is not equally well preserved everywhere.
●●●Well established

Radiometric dating of the boundary

What it is
High-precision ages from volcanic ash and impact glass close to the boundary.
What it helps us understand
Places the extinction and the impact at about 66 million years ago, close together in time.
How it is studied
Argon–argon and uranium–lead dating.
What it cannot tell us
Precision is high, but resolving the exact sequence of impact and volcanic pulses remains challenging.

Why carbon dating can't date dinosaurs

works only for once-living material up to around 50,000 years old, because carbon-14 decays too quickly to measure beyond that. Dinosaur fossils are tens of millions of years old. They are dated using other isotopes with much longer half-lives, usually from volcanic layers above and below the fossils.

Glossary

Mesozoic Era

The era from about 252 to 66 million years ago, divided into the Triassic, Jurassic and Cretaceous periods.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

iridium anomaly

An unusually high concentration of the metal iridium in a thin rock layer. Iridium is rare in Earth's crust but more common in many asteroids.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

shocked quartz

Quartz grains with microscopic deformation features formed only under extreme, sudden pressure, such as a large impact.

Open the full glossary

Glossary

radiocarbon dating

Dating once-living material from the decay of carbon-14. It works back to around 50,000 years at most, so it cannot date dinosaur fossils.
