Evidence and methods

How do we know?

Every date and claim in this exhibition rests on evidence that someone gathered, tested and argued about. This guide explains the main methods, what each one can tell us, and where each one stops.

01 · How can a rock have an age in years?

Radiometric dating

Typical reach: Thousands to billions of years, depending on the isotope

How it works
Some atoms are unstable and decay into other atoms at a steady, measurable rate, described by a half-life. Measuring how much of the original (parent) isotope remains compared with what it has decayed into (the daughter) gives the time since the mineral formed. Isotopes with very long half-lives are used for very old rocks.
What it can tell us
Numeric ages for volcanic rocks and minerals, and the age of Earth itself: about 4.54 billion years, from lead isotopes in meteorites.
Where it stops
It dates when a mineral crystallised, not when a fossil animal lived, so fossils are usually dated by the volcanic layers above and below them. Samples can be altered by later heating or weathering, which is why results are cross-checked between methods and laboratories.

Used in: Chapter 2, A Planet Is Born, Chapter 5, The Age of Giants

02 · Why can't carbon dating date a dinosaur?

Radiocarbon dating

Typical reach: Up to about 50,000 years

How it works
Living things take in carbon, including a tiny amount of radioactive carbon-14. When an organism dies, it stops taking in carbon and its carbon-14 decays. Measuring how much remains gives the time since death.
What it can tell us
Ages for once-living material such as bone, charcoal, wood, seeds and textiles: the tool behind much of the dating of the last tens of thousands of years, including early farming and the earliest cities.
Where it stops
After about 50,000 years too little carbon-14 is left to measure, so it cannot date dinosaurs or most of human evolution. Results must be calibrated, because the amount of carbon-14 in the atmosphere has varied over time.

Used in: Chapter 6, The Human Story Begins, Chapter 7, The Birth of Civilisation

03 · How can layers of rock put events in order?

Stratigraphy and relative dating

Typical reach: Any age where layers survive

How it works
In undisturbed layers of rock or soil, deeper layers were laid down first, so they are older. Archaeologists and geologists record which layer each find came from and match layers between sites, sometimes using distinctive fossils.
What it can tell us
The order of events, even when no numeric age is possible. Combined with radiometric or radiocarbon dates from some layers, it puts numeric ages on whole sequences.
Where it stops
Layers can be folded, eroded, dug into or mixed, and a missing layer can hide a long gap in time. Relative dating says 'older than' or 'younger than'; numeric ages need another method.

Used in: Chapter 4, When Life Diversified, Chapter 5, The Age of Giants, Chapter 7, The Birth of Civilisation

04 · What can a fossil tell us, and what is missing?

Fossils and taphonomy

Typical reach: From over 3 billion years ago to the recent past

How it works
Fossils are the preserved remains or traces of past life: bones, shells, leaves, footprints, burrows and microbial structures. Taphonomy is the study of what happens to remains between death and discovery.
What it can tell us
What lived, when (from the layers they are found in), and often how it moved, grew and fed. The order of fossils in rock layers shows how life changed over time.
Where it stops
Most organisms decay or are destroyed before they can fossilise, and soft-bodied life is rarely preserved. The record is a biased sample: it favours hard parts, certain environments and certain times, so absence of a fossil is not proof of absence.

Used in: Chapter 3, The First Breath of Life, Chapter 4, When Life Diversified, Chapter 5, The Age of Giants, Chapter 6, The Human Story Begins

05 · How do we know what past climates were like?

Ice and sediment cores

Typical reach: Ice: hundreds of thousands of years. Ocean and lake sediments: far longer

How it works
Snow that piles up on ice sheets traps air bubbles and records conditions year after year. Drilled ice cores and cores of ocean and lake mud are 'proxy' records: they do not measure temperature directly, but preserve clues that track it, alongside other proxies such as tree rings and corals.
What it can tell us
Past temperatures, precipitation, the composition of the atmosphere including carbon dioxide, volcanic eruptions and wind patterns.
Where it stops
Proxies have to be calibrated against modern measurements, and each records a particular place. Their time resolution blurs further back in time.

Used in: Chapter 2, A Planet Is Born, Chapter 9, The World We Built, Chapter 10, And Then There Was You

06 · How can genes reveal history?

Ancient DNA and molecular clocks

Typical reach: Ancient DNA: limited by how long DNA survives. Molecular clocks: any age, with calibration

How it works
DNA recovered from ancient bones can be sequenced and compared with living people. Molecular clocks estimate when two lineages split by counting the differences between their DNA and applying a rate of change, calibrated against dated fossils.
What it can tell us
Relationships between populations and species, including that many people today carry a small share of Neanderthal DNA. Estimates of when lineages diverged.
Where it stops
DNA breaks down over time, so old samples are rare and often damaged. Rates of genetic change vary, so clock estimates depend on their calibrations and come with wide ranges.

Used in: Chapter 6, The Human Story Begins, Chapter 3, The First Breath of Life

07 · How can we know what happened 13.8 billion years ago?

Cosmic light: background radiation and redshift

Typical reach: Back to about 380,000 years after the Big Bang (directly visible light)

How it works
Light from distant galaxies is stretched to longer, redder wavelengths as space expands: the farther the galaxy, the greater the redshift. The cosmic microwave background is light released when the universe became transparent, about 380,000 years after the Big Bang; its pattern can be mapped across the whole sky.
What it can tell us
That the universe is expanding, how fast, and, from detailed maps of the background light, its age of about 13.8 billion years and its contents.
Where it stops
Nothing earlier than the background light can be seen directly; earlier stages are inferred from models.

Used in: Chapter 1, Before Everything

08 · How do historians test what old texts say?

Manuscripts, inscriptions and source criticism

Typical reach: Roughly the last 5,000 years, since writing began

How it works
Historians date and test written sources by their material (clay, parchment, birch bark, paper), their script and language, and sometimes by radiocarbon dating the material itself. They ask who wrote a text, when, for whom and why, and compare independent sources.
What it can tell us
Names, events, beliefs and everyday records such as accounts and rations, often from the people involved.
Where it stops
Most people in the past left no writing, and texts reflect the views of those who wrote them. Copies may change over time, and a material date gives the age of the material, not necessarily of the text.

Used in: Chapter 7, The Birth of Civilisation, Chapter 8, The Age of Ideas

Labels used across the exhibition

Reading the evidence labels

Evidence cards and timeline events carry a label saying how settled the question is. The label always appears as words and a symbol, never as colour alone.

●●●Well established
Supported by many independent lines of evidence; not seriously disputed among specialists.
●●○Strong evidence
Well supported, though details or interpretations are still being refined.
●○○Active research
Evidence exists, but new findings are changing the picture.
◐Debated
Specialists disagree about how to interpret the evidence.
○Hypothesis
A proposed explanation that has not yet been well tested.
?Unknown
There is not yet enough evidence to say.

A note on numbers

Living with uncertainty

Dates in this exhibition are rounded to the precision the evidence supports. “About 66 million years ago” is a precise measurement with a small margin of error; “roughly 12,000 to 10,000 years ago” for the start of farming in Southwest Asia is a range, because the change happened over a long span, not in a moment. Words like about, roughly and at least are there on purpose.

“Years ago” on the timeline counts back from the year 2000. Radiocarbon laboratories count “before present” from 1950 instead. At the scale of millions of years the difference does not matter; for the last few centuries, calendar years are given as well.

When new evidence changes a date or an interpretation, that is the method working. Many of the ideas in this exhibition were revised in living memory, and some will be revised again.
